Reading Reflection: On Photography by Susan Sontag

I'm used to thinking about the world around me all the time and rarely pay attention to the process of taking a photo. Sometimes there is just an emotion, and I try to catch that something which I experienced and want to remember. My photos are mostly captured with my phone. I can photograph something interesting for me, take pictures of friends, take a selfie or photograph some place I want to remember. Since taking a photo is a simple and quick procedure, it is so much easier to do it. "In Plato's Cave" by Susan Sontag made me think a bit differently about this process and how the world becomes an image in the moment of creating a photograph. According to Sontag, photography is the process of collecting the world and making experiences turn into the object which could be collected, shared and viewed after. And photographs are never totally impartial. What gets into the frame, what is left outside of it and timing of shooting. Therefore, in this context, a photograph could become a recording of reality but also could be considered as an interpretation of it. I believe that this idea also shows up in casual photography. When I take a picture, I do not just preserve that moment. I decide which moment will be preserved. I see this point of view most relevant while using social media. People photograph the best moments of their lives which are the most attractive, fascinating and unusual for them in order to avoid boredom, stress and monotony of everyday life. As a result, they create a collection of photos making their lives look more polished than they actually are. In addition to the topic of photography, Susan Sontag also talks about the usage of cameras in the process of tourism and how travelers use cameras in order to collect photographs of places they have visited. This aspect is still relevant to me since sometimes documenting an experience is as important as having it. If I had to visualize our present without using words, I would focus on contradictions. For instance, one picture could describe a group of people looking at a different phone screen. Another picture could represent a landscape but with pollution, construction or extreme weather. A person surrounded by hundreds of photos, notifications, advertisements and videos could be an example of how difficult it is to distinguish real information from an endless flow of visuals. I would also like to visualize this relationship we have between us and memory. We take so many pictures because we are afraid of forgetting so many things and the large number of pictures makes each of them less significant. According to Susan Sontag, photography makes us feel the world is more available for us than it actually is. This point seems particularly relevant nowadays. It's the image making mechanism which helps us remember, communicate and understand the world; we should also ask ourselves which we see and what is left outside the frame.

Reading Reflection: Your Brain on Chat GPT

The YouTube video on the influence of ChatGPT on the brain, combined with the research "Your Brain on ChatGPT" by the MIT Media Lab, raised many questions concerning the ways artificial intelligence changes the approaches to learning, writing, and problem-solving. Previously, I considered ChatGPT as one of the tools that make working more convenient and fast. The most interesting one is the difference in the level of brain connectivity of the three groups: people who wrote essays on their own, people who used search engines, and people who used ChatGPT. Based on the results, the first group has shown the highest connectivity, while the second group has demonstrated the weakest connectivity. Another interesting aspect is that some of the participants from the group who used the program found it more difficult to recall or quote what they have written. This finding was interesting to me since it indicates that the completion of an assignment and learning from an assignment are two different things. The YouTube video continued this line of reasoning and discussed the possibility of reducing the number of mental efforts required by constantly delegating the difficulties of the task to AI. The thing that came to my mind would be the need to strengthen your brain with knowledge like you would strengthen your muscles. Skills like writing, remembering, analyzing, and solving problems require constant exercise and if a person starts putting all of those challenging activities immediately to AI, they may lose the opportunity to develop those skills. That is why I believe ChatGPT is not necessarily harmful. I find the main point of both sources is the ways the technology is applied. For example, there is a significant difference between making the AI complete the whole assignment and using it for explanations, feedback, or discussion of ideas previously created by the person. In the latter case, the application of AI can contribute to more intensive learning, and not prevent it. There are clear boundaries to this I believe things like ai solely for art takes away from the learning completely because in this scenario you are putting all the challenging tasks to the ai and taking away any intentionality , but for more technical things like coding you can have it develop a base framework for you to learn and guide from, in this scenario its like a calculator it still needs your guidance, info and knowledge otherwise the final piece or numbers don't come as you intended. For me, the key takeaway from the research is that AI should be used as an additional resource and not as a substitute for thinking. The technology is very helpful; however, its convenience can become a problem, if the process of learning is stripped from the effort. I believe that AI should be used mindfully and not used without a second thought because. struggles, independent ideas, and making mistakes are valuable parts of learning and ChatGPT and other AI models can contribute to the process, but it should not dominate it completely.

Reading Reflection: Motherhood in Crisis by Laurence Ivil, Alicia Prager and Saidu Bah

The Motherhood in Crisis series published by Al Jazeera includes an individual story called Memunatu: The distant patient ‘Birth in my village’ told with the help of illustrations, texts, photography, and video recordings. Personally, I find the illustrations more effective in terms of portraying physical and psychological challenges of giving birth experienced by a woman in the rural part of Sierra Leone. In my opinion, illustration is quite useful for showing what happened some time ago and adds to the retelling of a past event. As opposed to just descriptions, the illustrations show Memunatu's life during and after childbirth in an expressive way. I think that the most distinctive feature of illustrations is the difference in style compared to photographs. Firstly, the illustrations in this story have characteristic features of a graphic novel, including thick outlines. Secondly, the colours used to illustrate Memunatu's difficult childbirth have a cool blue color using the night sky to help show when Memunatu was at her lowest point. In addition, the facial expressions of Memunatu are somewhat exaggerated in order to show her feelings better. In addition to that, one of the advantages of illustration is the possibility to tell about sensitive or even traumatic experiences without using pictures which might upset viewers. In this story, Memunatu goes through labour, loses her baby, and develops a fistula. By illustrating these experiences, it is possible to tell the viewers about the severity of these events while also preserving Memunatu's dignity and privacy. I also believe that the comic-book-like style of illustration is more interesting than only the text and lets readers follow all the events easily. At the end of the story, we can also see several photographs. Unlike the illustrations which are reproductions of memories, the photographs include actual people, environment, and medical facilities. Like when Memunatu returns to the Aberdeen Women's Centre holding her baby the viewers get to know that she was able to overcome her problems in childbirth and there is hope. The transition from illustrations to photographs also makes the story feel closer to the reader; there is even one part where there are illustrations of people with a real photo as the background. The video recording of Dr Hawanatu Barrie brings another layer of closeness to the story. Listening to a doctor talking about the dangers of prolonged labour and obstetric fistula provides factual information to support Memunatu's personal story. While the illustrations help to communicate emotions and reproduce memories, photographs and video help to see reality and communicate with people. Overall, I think that the variety of visual communication approaches used in this story makes it more effective than just retelling in text alone.The illustrations make the viewers empathize. On the other hand, photographs and video help to show the reality and make it feel closer to the reader as illustrations can be exaggerated and diverge from reality and using both illustrations and photographs helps fill for the weaknesses that can come from doing only one.
